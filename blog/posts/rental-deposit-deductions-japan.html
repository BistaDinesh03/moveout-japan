Rental Deposit Deductions in Japan: What Landlords Can and Cannot Charge

Published October 23, 2026  |  Moving Out  |  13 min read

The deposit is one of the few parts of a Japanese tenancy where the rules are clearer than the arguments suggest. Ordinary wear and aging are generally the landlord's cost. Damage caused by intent, negligence, or use beyond ordinary living is generally the tenant's. Most disputes are not about those principles — they are about which category a mark belongs to, whether a special clause is valid, and whether anyone explained the deduction. This guide works through those questions using the Civil Code, the national consumer centre's published cases, and the transport ministry's restoration guideline.

⚠️ General information, not legal advice. This article describes Japanese law and official guidance as published on 23 October 2026. Contracts, buildings, and facts differ, and a guideline is not a substitute for advice on your case. For a live dispute, consult a local consumer centre or a qualified lawyer.

The Short Answer

You are not automatically liable for repainting, wallpaper replacement, tatami replacement, or a standard move-out cleaning fee. Under the Civil Code, the tenant is responsible for damage caused after taking over the property, but ordinary wear from normal use and ordinary aging are excluded. The transport ministry's guideline puts the same principle in practical terms: ordinary wear and aging are part of what rent pays for, while damage from intentional acts, negligence, a failure of the tenant's duty of care, or use beyond ordinary living is the tenant's responsibility.

A special clause can change that starting point, but not every clause works. The clause has to be specific about what the tenant is taking on, it has to be understood and agreed to, and it must not be an exploitative term that the law refuses to enforce. A cleaning fee can be valid for the same reason, but a vague "tenant pays cleaning" line is weaker than a specific fee with a defined scope.

The deposit itself is not a fee the landlord keeps. Article 622-2 of the Civil Code says the landlord returns it after the lease ends and the property is handed back, minus money obligations owed under the lease. The practical question is what those obligations are, and that is why the itemised statement matters.

💡 Keep this distinction: "restoration" does not mean "put the apartment back exactly as it was when you moved in." The ministry guideline explicitly says restoration is not a return to the original condition. It is about repairing damage that is attributable to you, with age and ordinary wear taken into account.

Why does this keep turning into a dispute? The National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan (NCAC) counted 14,711 consultations about rental restoration problems in the 2025 fiscal year, and 1,846 in the 2026 fiscal year through 31 May 2026 — up from 1,645 in the same period a year earlier. On 17 February 2026 it published a fresh alert with real consultation examples, including a sofa mark on wallpaper, a demand for full wallpaper and tatami replacement, and air-conditioner cleaning charged at both move-in and move-out. Those are complaints, not court rulings, but they show the shape of the arguments.

1. What "Restoration" Actually Means

The legal starting point is Article 621 of the Civil Code. In the Ministry of Justice's English translation, it says that if damage is caused to the leased thing after the tenant receives it, the tenant has an obligation to restore it when the lease ends. But the same article excludes wear caused by the ordinary manner of using and making profit from the property and aging degradation, and it does not apply where the damage is due to grounds not attributable to the tenant.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) guideline translates that into a working definition. It describes restoration as repairing the loss in building value caused by the tenant's intentional act, negligence, breach of the duty of care, or other use beyond ordinary use. Ordinary aging and wear from normal use are treated as covered by rent. The guideline also says, plainly, that restoration is not the same as returning the property to its original state.

The guideline is guidance rather than a statute, but it is the ministry's published standard and the consumer centre points tenants to it. It is also long — the download page describes a 173-page document — so the Q&A page is often the more useful entry point. The Q&A covers the questions that come up in real move-outs: special clauses, wallpaper, tatami and fusuma, deposit timing, inspection signatures, cleaning clauses, and requests for a breakdown.

📜 Official sources: Civil Code, Article 621 (English translation, Ministry of Justice) and MLIT's overview of the restoration guideline (Japanese).

2. Ordinary Wear vs Damage

The guideline does not give a universal list of every mark that is ordinary wear. It gives a test: was the loss caused by the tenant's intentional act, negligence, breach of the duty of care, or use beyond ordinary living? If it was, the tenant is responsible. If it was ordinary use or aging, the landlord is. In practice, that means the same kind of mark can land on different sides of the line depending on how it happened, how old the surface was, and what the tenant did about it.

🏠 Generally ordinary wear and aging

Kawasaki City's consumer centre gives these examples of deterioration that occurs through normal living: sun-faded wallpaper and tatami, indentations in flooring or carpet from furniture, and dark marks on the wall behind a television or refrigerator. The MLIT guideline treats ordinary wear and aging as the landlord's cost because rent already pays for it.

🔨 Generally damage beyond ordinary use

The same Kawasaki page says tobacco tar and smell, and scratches on pillars caused by pets, are not ordinary wear or aging. The MLIT Q&A also gives examples of tenant responsibility: mould that requires special cleaning because ordinary cleaning was neglected, and stains left from spilled drinks or condensation. A leak the tenant failed to report, allowing damage to spread downstairs, is another example the Q&A uses.

The grey areas are where most money is argued over. A sofa mark on wallpaper may be ordinary wear if it came from normal furniture placement; a cigarette burn is not. A small scuff near a door may be ordinary living; a hole punched in the wall is not. Old wallpaper that fades may be at the end of its life; the same wallpaper torn by a pet is a different question. The guideline's approach is to look at the cause and the age of the part, not just at whether a mark exists.

NCAC's 2026 alert shows how quickly the labels can be contested. In one consultation, a tenant who had lived in a flat for six years was told that a sofa mark on the wallpaper required replacement; the landlord offered to waive the wallpaper material because of the six years but still asked for a share of the labour. In another, a tenant who had lived in a flat for about ten years was asked for full wallpaper and tatami replacement on top of a deposit deduction, despite a contract clause the tenant said had never been explained. In a third, a tenant was charged for air-conditioner cleaning at move-out after already paying for it at move-in. NCAC's advice in each case starts from the same point: ordinary wear and aging are generally not the tenant's cost, and an unexplained or overbroad clause is not the end of the conversation.

One caution about those examples: they are summaries of consultations published by NCAC, not judgments. They are useful because they show what landlords actually claim and what tenants actually ask, not because they guarantee the same result in your building.

3. Depreciation and Repair Scope

Even when you are responsible for damage, you are not normally paying for a brand-new apartment. The guideline says the tenant's share should take account of how many years each part has been in use, with the burden decreasing as the years pass. The MLIT Q&A puts it directly in the wallpaper example: the repair should be considered in light of the elapsed years for each part, and the work should be kept to the smallest practical unit.

That last point matters. If you damage one section of wallpaper, the starting point is not "replace every wall in the apartment." The guideline says the smallest practical repair unit is preferred, although replacing one wall surface that contains the damaged area may sometimes be unavoidable to match the pattern. The same logic applies to tatami, fusuma, and shoji: ordinary wear does not make the tenant pay for replacement, and if the tenant did damage them, the focus is on the damaged sheets or panels rather than the whole room.

The Q&A also addresses unit prices. Restoration costs vary with the materials and construction method, so a price from a previous apartment is not automatically the right benchmark. But the tenant is not supposed to pay for an upgrade: the Q&A says the cost should be based on restoring the condition with age and ordinary wear taken into account, and the tenant does not have to bear the cost of installing higher-grade wallpaper. If a quote looks high, ask for the material, labour, and age adjustment to be separated.

📈 Ask the age question early. "How old was the wallpaper, tatami, or carpet, and how does the quote account for that?" is a more useful question than "Why is this so expensive?" It forces the calculation back to the guideline's framework: cause, age, and the smallest practical repair.

4. When a Special Clause Can Shift the Cost

Japanese lease contracts often contain a tokuyaku (特約), a special clause that changes the default rule. A clause can, in principle, put some ordinary-wear costs on the tenant. But the MLIT Q&A is clear that a clause that is disadvantageous to the tenant is not valid merely because it is written down. The tenant must understand the content and agree to make it part of the contract.

The Q&A sets out three conditions that are generally required for a tenant-disadvantageous restoration clause to be enforceable:

  1. There is a real reason for the clause, and it is not exploitative. The clause needs an objective, reasonable basis — not just a standard form that shifts every cost to the tenant.
  2. The tenant recognises that the clause goes beyond the ordinary restoration obligation. It is not enough to bury the point in a long contract; the tenant must understand that they are taking on costs that would otherwise be the landlord's.
  3. The tenant expresses an intention to take on that obligation. Agreement must be real, not assumed from a signature on a document nobody explained.

Kawasaki City's consumer centre describes the same three requirements, and adds that the 2020 Civil Code reform did not codify special clauses. It says the courts' accumulated requirements still matter. The Q&A also notes that the Consumer Contract Act can invalidate a clause that unfairly harms the consumer, and that a clause fixing a specific damage amount in advance is possible but not always enforceable; the actual loss may still matter.

There is a timing question too. Kawasaki City explains that the amended Civil Code applies to leases concluded on or after 1 April 2020. An older lease that the parties agree to renew after that date can be covered by the new rules, while an automatic statutory renewal is generally treated under the old rules. If you have lived in the same place for years, the date and type of renewal can matter as much as the clause itself.

📝 What to ask before you sign: "Please show me the exact restoration and cleaning clauses, explain what they add beyond the legal default, and confirm the amount or method." If the answer is "it is standard," ask for the clause in writing and for an explanation in a language you understand. A clause you did not understand is a weaker clause, but it is still better to resolve the question before signing than after you have moved out.

5. Cleaning Fees and Fixed Charges

Cleaning is the most common fixed charge in a Japanese move-out, and it is also the one most often misunderstood in both directions. A cleaning clause is not automatically illegal, and it is not automatically valid. The MLIT Q&A says validity is judged on three points: whether the content and scope of what the tenant must bear are stated; whether the clause makes clear that the tenant is also bearing ordinary-wear cleaning, and what that concrete scope is, in writing or in an oral explanation; and whether the amount is reasonable.

The Q&A gives court examples on both sides. One court upheld a clearly agreed clause requiring professional cleaning at the end of the lease, with a specific fee, where the content had been explained and the amount was reasonable in context. Another court held that a general clause saying "the tenant bears the cost of room cleaning" did not, by itself, establish that the tenant had agreed to bear ordinary wear as well. The lesson is not "all cleaning fees are invalid" or "all cleaning fees are valid." The lesson is that specificity and explanation do the work.

NCAC's 2026 alert adds a practical warning about double charging. In one consultation, a tenant had paid an air-conditioner cleaning fee at move-in, then was charged another air-conditioner cleaning fee at move-out; the important-matters document said the tenant bore both. Whether that is valid depends on the contract and the explanation, but the first step is to compare the move-in receipt, the contract, and the move-out invoice. If you paid for a professional clean at move-in and were told that move-out cleaning was included, keep that document.

Ask what the fee actually covers. A "cleaning fee" may include the kitchen, bath, toilet, ventilation, blinds, air conditioner, carpet, or tatami — or only some of them. Ask whether it is a fixed fee or an estimate of actual work, whether it is charged even if the apartment is left clean, and whether it overlaps with a separate restoration charge for the same item. A specific, explained fee for a defined scope is easier to evaluate than a single lump sum with no breakdown.

6. The Inspection and Your Signature

Attend the move-out inspection if you can. Bring your phone, the contract, your move-in photos, and a notebook. Walk through each room and compare the condition with the photos you took when you moved in. Record what is new, what was already there, and what is ordinary wear. If the landlord or agent points to damage, ask them to describe it precisely and to note it on the sheet in neutral language — not just "damage" or "dirty."

The signature is where many tenants panic. The MLIT Q&A addresses this directly. If the inspection sheet merely confirms that damage exists, that is not automatically agreement to bear the restoration cost. If you acknowledged that you would bear the cost for specific damage, that acknowledgement can carry weight. But damage that was not caused by your intent or negligence does not become your responsibility merely because you signed a condition report, unless a valid special clause says otherwise. The Q&A also advises tenants to ask questions and proceed carefully, because the inspection is important evidence for both sides.

Practical translation: sign that you attended and that the listed observations are what you saw, if that is true. Do not sign a blank sheet or a sheet that omits your comments. If you disagree with a characterisation, ask for your note to be added or write a separate record and email it the same day. Ask for a copy of everything you sign. If you cannot attend, ask whether a representative can attend, and ask for dated photos or a video of the final condition.

📷 Your best evidence is boring: dated move-in photos, dated move-out photos, the inspection sheet, the contract, and every email. Take a short video walking through each room on the day you hand back the keys. It is faster than arguing about a mark six weeks later.

7. Ask for an Itemised Statement

You do not have to accept a single number. The MLIT Q&A says the landlord has an obligation to explain restoration costs deducted from the deposit, and the tenant can request the contents and breakdown. That is the lever to use before you pay or agree to a deduction.

A useful breakdown separates:

  • the item and location — which wall, floor, tatami mat, air conditioner, or fixture;
  • the work — repair, replacement, cleaning, or disposal, and how much of it is actually needed;
  • the quantity and unit price — square metres, metres, sheets, units, or hours, plus labour and tax;
  • the age adjustment — how the elapsed years for that part were taken into account;
  • the clause — the exact contract term the landlord relies on, and why it covers this item;
  • the evidence — photos, a quote, or an invoice from the contractor.

If the statement is a lump sum, ask for it to be reissued with the items separated. If the landlord says the contractor's quote is confidential, ask for the scope and the amount attributable to your alleged damage, with the rest redacted if necessary. You are not asking for the contractor's trade secrets; you are asking for the basis of a payment you are being asked to make.

The Q&A also notes that restoration unit prices vary by property and method, so a higher price than a friend paid is not automatically wrong. The better questions are whether the work is within the smallest practical repair, whether the age of the part was considered, and whether you are being charged for an upgrade. Those are the points the guideline actually addresses.

8. When the Deposit Must Come Back

Article 622-2 of the Civil Code defines a security deposit as money delivered by the tenant to the landlord, under any name, to secure the tenant's money obligations under the lease. It says the landlord must return the amount that remains after deducting the money obligations owed by the tenant under the lease in two situations: when the lease ends and the landlord receives the property back, or when the tenant lawfully assigns the lease.

The Code does not set a single number of days for the refund. The MLIT Q&A says that unless the contract specifies a different time, the tenant's claim for return arises after the property has been handed over, not at the moment the lease ends. That is why the handover date, the inspection, and the final meter readings matter: they start the practical clock.

Check your contract for a settlement period. If it has one, that is the date to diary. If it does not, ask the landlord in writing when the final statement will be issued and when the balance will be paid. If you disagree with part of the deduction, say so in writing and identify the parts you accept and the parts you dispute. Silence is not a strategy: it makes it harder to show later that you objected.

One more point on the deposit's purpose: it secures money obligations, not every possible complaint. The Code speaks of the amount owed under the lease; it does not create a general right to keep the deposit because the landlord is disappointed with the condition. The itemised statement is how you test whether each deduction is actually an obligation you owe.

9. How to Dispute a Deduction

Start with a written request, not a phone argument. A short, factual email is enough: ask for the itemised statement, the contract clause, the photos, and the age calculation. Say which items you accept, which you dispute, and why. Attach your move-in and move-out photos. Keep the tone neutral; the email may be read by someone who was not at the inspection.

If the reply does not resolve it, compare the statement with the guideline's framework: Was the damage caused by you, or is it ordinary wear? If you caused it, was the repair kept to the smallest practical unit? Was the age of the part considered? Is the clause specific, explained, and reasonable? Is the same item being charged twice? These questions turn a general complaint into a set of points the other side has to answer.

In Japan, the national Consumer Hotline 188 connects you to a local consumer centre. NCAC's alert recommends consulting a consumer centre when a restoration charge is not acceptable, and its topic page collects the consultation trends. The centre can advise on the dispute and on how to communicate with the landlord; it is not a court and cannot order a refund, but it is an official starting point. If you are in Japan, call 188. If you may be abroad before the dispute is resolved, ask the centre before you leave what it can offer once you are outside Japan.

For unresolved money claims, the MLIT Q&A describes civil mediation and the small-claims procedure. It says the small-claims route covers money claims up to ¥600,000 and is designed, in principle, for a single hearing. That is a useful option for a deposit dispute that is large enough to matter but too small for a full trial. For a large or complex case, or if the legal issue is unclear, get qualified advice before you choose a procedure.

10. If You Are Leaving Japan

A deposit dispute is harder once you are on the other side of the world, so handle the money questions before you leave. Ask the landlord in writing how the refund will be paid, what bank details they need, and when they expect to issue the final statement. If you are closing your Japanese bank account, agree an alternative before you close it; if you can keep the account open until the refund arrives, that removes one moving part.

Mail is the other trap. Japan Post's forwarding service redirects post to a new address in Japan; it does not forward mail overseas. If the final statement or refund notice is sent by post after you leave, it may never reach you. Give the landlord an email address and a reliable contact, and ask for documents by email as well. Our administrative checklist for leaving Japan covers mail forwarding, the municipal notifications, tax, pension, and health insurance — the paperwork that runs alongside the apartment handover.

If you cannot attend the inspection, ask in advance whether a representative can attend and whether the landlord will provide dated photos or a video. Get the inspection sheet and the final statement by email. Keep digital copies of the contract, move-in photos, move-out photos, the inspection sheet, the deduction statement, and every email in one folder. If the dispute is still open when you leave, tell the landlord in writing that you are disputing the specific items and give an address where you can be reached.

11. A Practical Checklist

The order matters more than the wording. Evidence collected early is worth more than an argument later.

📄 Before you sign the lease

  • Read the restoration and cleaning clauses, not just the rent and deposit figures.
  • Ask what the clause adds beyond the legal default and whether the amount is fixed or estimated.
  • Ask for an explanation in a language you understand, and keep a copy.
  • Check whether the lease is new, renewed by agreement, or automatically renewed.

📷 When you move in

  • Photograph every room, including walls, floors, tatami, fixtures, and appliances.
  • Note existing marks, stains, holes, and odours; date the record.
  • Test the air conditioner, water heater, ventilation, and other fixtures.
  • Keep the condition report and your photos somewhere you can find them years later.

🛠 During the tenancy

  • Report leaks, mould, and broken fixtures promptly; do not let a small problem spread.
  • Do not make unauthorised repairs; ask the landlord first.
  • Keep receipts for anything you paid for that relates to the property.
  • If you have pets, check the contract and keep the pet damage issue in mind.

🗑 Before the handover

  • Clean to an ordinary domestic standard; ordinary wear is not yours to restore to new.
  • Take dated photos and a short video after cleaning and after the furniture is out.
  • Compare the condition with your move-in photos and list the differences.
  • Ask for the inspection date, the final statement date, and the refund method in writing.

✍️ At the inspection

  • Attend if possible; bring the contract and your photos.
  • Ask for neutral descriptions and for your comments to be recorded.
  • Do not sign a blank or incomplete sheet; get a copy of what you sign.
  • If you are pressured, say you will review the sheet and respond in writing.

💰 After the handover

  • Request the itemised statement, the clause, the photos, and the age calculation.
  • Identify the items you accept and the items you dispute in writing.
  • Diary the settlement date in the contract, if there is one.
  • Call 188 for a local consumer centre if negotiation stalls; ask about mediation or small claims.

If you are leaving Japan, add one more item to the list: make sure the landlord can reach you and pay you after you go. A forwarding address that works, an email address you check, and a bank account or agreed alternative are part of the move-out, not an afterthought.

Frequently Asked Questions About Deposit Deductions

Can my landlord charge me for normal wear and tear in Japan? +

Generally, no. Article 621 of the Civil Code says the tenant must restore damage caused after taking over the property, but it excludes wear from ordinary use and ordinary aging, and damage not attributable to the tenant. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism guideline says ordinary wear and aging are covered by rent. A valid special clause can shift some of that cost to the tenant, but a clause is not automatically enforceable just because it is printed in the contract.

Is a fixed cleaning fee automatically taken from my deposit? +

Not automatically. The MLIT Q&A says a cleaning clause is judged on whether the tenant's scope of responsibility is stated, whether any ordinary-wear portion and its concrete scope were explained in writing or orally, and whether the amount is reasonable. A specific, explained, reasonable move-out cleaning fee can be valid; a vague clause that simply says the tenant pays for room cleaning may not cover ordinary wear.

Does signing the inspection sheet mean I agree to the repair costs? +

Not necessarily. The MLIT Q&A says that if the sheet merely confirms that damage exists, that is not automatically agreement to bear the restoration cost. If you acknowledged that you would bear the cost for specific damage, that acknowledgement can carry weight. Damage that was not caused by your intent or negligence does not become your responsibility merely because you signed a condition report, unless a valid special clause says otherwise.

How long does a landlord have to return my deposit in Japan? +

Article 622-2 of the Civil Code says the landlord must return the deposit, less money obligations owed under the lease, after the lease ends and the landlord receives the property back. The Code does not set a single number of days. The MLIT Q&A says that unless the contract sets a different time, the tenant's claim arises after handover. Check your contract for a settlement period and ask for the calculation in writing.

Can I ask for an itemised breakdown of the deductions? +

Yes. The MLIT Q&A says the landlord has an obligation to explain restoration costs deducted from the deposit, and the tenant can request the contents and breakdown. Ask for each item, the room or part affected, the quantity, unit price, labour, tax, any age or depreciation adjustment, and the contract clause relied on. Keep the request and the reply in writing.

What can I do if I disagree with a deposit deduction? +

Ask for the itemised calculation and the contract clause in writing, compare it with your move-in photos and the condition at handover, and set out which items you accept and which you dispute. In Japan, the national Consumer Hotline 188 connects you to a local consumer centre, which can advise on the dispute. The MLIT Q&A also describes civil mediation and small-claims procedures for money claims. For a large or complex dispute, get qualified legal advice.

Does the 2020 Civil Code reform apply to an older lease? +

The amended Civil Code took effect on 1 April 2020. Kawasaki City's consumer centre explains that it applies to leases concluded on or after that date; an older lease that the parties agree to renew after that date can be covered, while an automatic statutory renewal is generally treated under the old rules. The date and type of renewal matter, so check the contract and ask a consumer centre if you are unsure.
