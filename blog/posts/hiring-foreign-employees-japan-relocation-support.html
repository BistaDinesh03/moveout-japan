Hiring Foreign Employees in Japan? A Practical Guide to Relocation, Housing and Settling-In Support

Published November 20, 2026  |  For Employers  |  25 min read

Signed offer, done. The harder part starts now: the employee needs somewhere to sleep, an address on a residence card, a way to reach the office, and someone who can explain which office does what. This guide is written for the people who own that problem — founders, HR managers, relocation leads and the providers they hire — and it separates what the rules require from what good organisations simply do well.

The Short Answer for Employers

If you are skimming, start here:

  • Separate three things: what your employee's residence status legally requires, what employment and labour rules require of you as an employer, and what support you choose to buy. They are different lists.
  • Specified Skilled Worker (i) is the exception that matters. The Immigration Services Agency lists ten forms of support that accepting companies are required to provide, and a support plan is part of the residence application. An accepting organisation may entrust the plan to a Registered Support Organization (ISA, Getting Company Support).
  • Other work statuses do not carry that same published ten-item plan. The official pages for Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services and Highly Skilled Professional list no equivalent support duty. Good onboarding is still good onboarding — just do not label it a statutory requirement.
  • Housing is the usual bottleneck. Guarantors, guarantee companies, upfront costs and lease explanations are unfamiliar to most new arrivals. The MLIT Apartment Search Guidebook is the single best free resource to put in an employee's hands.
  • Write the handover down. A one-page first-week schedule with named owners prevents most of the “I thought HR had done that” problems.
⚖️ Read this before acting: residence statuses, support plans and employer notifications depend on facts about your hire. Use the official sources linked in this article and confirm the current rules with the relevant authority or a qualified professional. Nothing here is a substitute for that check.

1. Before Your Employee Arrives

Most relocation problems are created before the employee boards a plane. Not through bad intent, but because six small decisions were never assigned to anyone. Work through these in order.

Employment and onboarding preparation

Put the basics in writing in a language the employee can understand: role, duties, place of work, working hours, pay, leave, probation, reporting line and start date. The Immigration Services Agency's pages for work statuses refer to the written statement of working conditions required under the Labour Standards Act, and the MHLW guideline on employment management of foreign workers covers clear documentation of working conditions, wages and hours, compliance with labour laws, social insurance, housing and treatment on termination. Some items in that guideline are effort obligations; the underlying labour laws are statutory.

Two practical additions help almost every international hire:

  • A named human being. Not a shared inbox. One person who answers “who do I ask?” for the first two weeks.
  • A written first-week schedule. Arrival, address registration, office visit, bank or payment setup, and a check-in at the end of week one. Leave the employee's own procedures to them, but make sure they know the deadline.

Confirm status of residence and work authorisation

The support an employee needs depends heavily on their status of residence. The Immigration Services Agency publishes official descriptions of Specified Skilled Worker, Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services and Highly Skilled Professional (Japanese-language pages). Do not assume that a visa category tells you everything about housing or banking: it tells you about permitted activities and status, not whether a landlord will accept the employee.

The MHLW foreign employment status notification is a separate employer obligation. Foreign employment must be notified; the MHLW Tokyo Labour Bureau's employer and foreign-worker page also points to consultation services that employers can use.

Accommodation arrangements

Decide before arrival whether the employee will move into company housing, a serviced apartment, a share house or a normal rental. Each choice has a different setup time, different paperwork and a different cost structure. Section 2 compares them. Whichever you choose, confirm the address is one the employee can use for their move-in notification — and check the building's own move-in and move-out rules early.

Arrival planning

  • Who meets the employee at the airport or station, and in which language?
  • Where do they go if the apartment is not ready? Who pays for those nights?
  • Will they have a phone with data on day one? A long-term contract often needs an address and a Japanese payment method; the employee-side moving-to-Japan guide covers the options in more detail.
  • Are the first few days' meals and transport sorted, at least approximately?

Communication and language support

An employee who cannot read a municipal letter will not know that there is a deadline. Decide which documents you will translate or explain, and who does it. For Guidebook on Living and Working in Japan, the Ministry of Justice publishes a free English PDF and editions in many other languages — useful as a reference you can hand over without writing your own guide.

Essential services

JETRO's Living in Japan page explains the public insurance schemes that apply to workers and notes that enrolment is mandatory, with employers required to enrol in many cases. It also states that workers' accident compensation insurance is paid entirely by the employer and must never be deducted from wages. Beyond insurance, check what the employee needs for electricity, gas, water, transport and a payment method, and be explicit about which of those the company arranges and which the employee handles.

2. Housing and Accommodation

Housing is where employer good intentions most often collide with Japanese rental practice. A landlord may require a joint guarantor or a guarantee company, upfront payments can be several months' rent, and the important-points explanation is a legal step that has to happen before signing. For the full employee-side explanation of that process, see our moving-to-Japan guide. This section focuses on the employer's decision.

Temporary accommodation

A hotel, serviced residence or monthly apartment buys time while screening finishes. It is not a long-term solution, but it removes the pressure to sign the first available lease. Ask whether the address can be used for resident registration, how mail is handled, and whether the provider will issue the documents a municipal office expects. Confirm the rules directly with the municipality if the answer is unclear.

Company housing

Where the employer holds the lease directly, the employee avoids the worst of the guarantor problem. This is also one way to meet the housing-related support item for Specified Skilled Worker (i) discussed in section 4. Set out in writing who occupies the property, who pays rent and utilities, what happens if employment ends, and how move-out cleaning and restoration are handled. Those details matter at the end of the assignment, not just at the start.

Normal rental arrangements

If the employee signs their own lease, expect them to need documents, a phone number, a payment method and possibly a guarantor or guarantee company. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism publishes an English Apartment Search Guidebook and a support page for foreign nationals looking for rental housing, including multilingual checklists and sample contracts. Give these to the employee rather than rewriting them.

Guarantors and guarantee companies

A joint guarantor is a person who takes on an obligation alongside the tenant. A rent guarantee company is a business that provides a guarantee under its own contract. MLIT operates a registration system for rent guarantee businesses, but the ministry describes it as voluntary — registration is not the same as a mandatory licence. MLIT also publishes a list of registered rent guarantee companies that provide language support for foreigners. Use it as a starting point, not a recommendation.

For real estate agents, the separate takken licence search confirms whether a business is licensed as a real estate transaction business. Do not confuse the two databases: takken is a broker licence; the guarantee-company register is a different, voluntary system.

Corporate housing providers

Corporate housing providers typically combine furnished accommodation, a lease structure a company can sign, and sometimes utility and cleaning arrangements. The useful question is not “are they good?” but “which exact problem do they remove for us?” Ask what is included, what the minimum term is, what happens if the assignment is extended or cut short, and who handles the end-of-tenancy clear-out when the employee leaves.

Questions to ask an accommodation partner

  • Can I see the full written cost breakdown, including taxes and optional items, before we commit?
  • Which lease type is this, and what happens at renewal or early termination?
  • Is a guarantor or guarantee company required, and who provide it?
  • Can the employee complete a move-in notification at this address?
  • Which utilities are already connected, and who signs the contracts?
  • What language support is available after the contract is signed, not only during the sale?
  • What is the process for a departing employee's furniture and appliance disposal?
  • Are you licensed or registered where the law requires it, and can you show the number?

  • The Ministry of Justice's Guidebook on Living and Working in Japan page, which links full guides in many languages.

4. What Support Is Required for Specified Skilled Worker (i)?

This is the section where precision matters most. The support regime below applies to employees with a Specified Skilled Worker (i) status of residence. It is not a general list of “things employers must do for foreign staff”.

🏦 Official position: the Immigration Services Agency states that foreign nationals working under a Specified Skilled Worker (i) status can receive ten forms of support from their company, and that companies are required to provide them. If the company cannot provide support, a registered support organization can provide it at the company's request. The agency also directs workers who feel they have not received proper support to the nearest Immigration office.

The ten official support categories

The ten forms of support listed by the Immigration Services Agency for SSW (i)
# Official support item What it means operationally
1 Pre-arrival explanation Explain the employment conditions and the support plan before the status-of-residence procedure, using a method the worker understands.
2 Airport pick-up and drop-off Arrange arrival transport and accompany the worker on departure, including to the airport security check.
3 Housing and contract assistance Help secure housing and sign contracts for electricity, gas, a bank account and a mobile phone. Acting as guarantor or providing company housing is one route.
4 Life orientation Explain Japanese rules and manners, public institutions, transport and disaster response.
5 Accompanying to public procedures Go with the worker to city hall and other offices for residence, social security and tax procedures, and help prepare documents.
6 Japanese-language learning opportunities Provide opportunities to study, including help finding a school where relevant.
7 Consultation and complaints Hold responsive meetings and give advice in a language the worker understands.
8 Interaction with Japanese people Create opportunities for local contact, such as community events.
9 Job-change support If the company intends to lay the worker off, help them find new employment, including time and documents for job hunting.
10 Regular meetings A support manager holds meetings at least once every three months and makes required reports to the authorities.

Source: ISA, Getting Company Support and the agency's Japanese support and Registered Support Organization page. The Japanese page explains that an accepting organisation prepares a support plan covering these items, submits it with the residence application, and notifies changes.

What the plan and the RSO system actually say

  • The accepting organisation prepares a support plan and can entrust all or part of it to a Registered Support Organization.
  • If the whole plan is entrusted, the accepting organisation is treated as meeting its support-structure standard.
  • An RSO may not sub-entrust the support work it has accepted.
  • RSO registration is with the Immigration Services Agency and is valid for a fixed term; the agency publishes a register that is updated as needed. The English list on the official RSO page was dated 19 November 2026 when checked.

Verify any prospective RSO against the official register — and remember that an RSO's duties are the support plan, not your employee's apartment clearance after they leave. That is a separate operational need.

What this does not mean for other statuses

The official ten-item regime is expressly tied to Specified Skilled Worker (i). The ISA pages for Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services and Highly Skilled Professional do not list an equivalent mandatory support plan. That does not make practical settling-in support pointless; it means you should describe it accurately as an onboarding choice, not as a statutory SSW-style duty. Because rules and individual cases change, confirm the current position for the status you are using.

5. Should Companies Manage Relocation Internally or Outsource It?

There is no single correct answer. The right model depends on how many people you hire, where they come from, whether any are SSW (i), and whether your team can cover languages and time zones. Most growing companies use a mixture.

Relocation support models compared (editorial analysis, not a ranking)
Model Strengths Limitations When it fits
Internal HR management Direct control, institutional knowledge, no procurement step Language and local knowledge gaps; staff time; single point of failure if one person leaves One or two hires a year, an office in the destination city, or an in-house international team
Relocation company One accountable provider for several tasks; local networks; can cover more than one city Scope and quality vary; some providers are sales-driven; “relocation” is not a regulated title Multiple hires, a new destination, or when HR lacks local language capacity
Registered Support Organization Specific statutory role for SSW (i); appears on an official register; can take all or part of the plan Registration confirms status, not service quality; cannot sub-entrust; focused on SSW (i) support, not every move-out task When you hire SSW (i) workers and want to entrust some or all of the support plan
Specialist housing provider Knows the rental market and lease paperwork; can offer company housing structures Housing only; may not handle admin, language or end-of-tenancy clearance When accommodation is the main bottleneck and the rest is managed elsewhere
Other service providers Targeted help with internet, mobile, translation, furniture or moving-out tasks Many small vendors to coordinate; quality must be checked individually As a flexible layer around an internal owner or a relocation provider

A useful rule of thumb: keep the decisions internal — who the employee is, what the support plan says, what you will pay for — and outsource the run of tasks where you lack local capacity. Outsourcing the decisions as well is how organisations end up paying for services they never needed.

6. How to Choose a Reliable Relocation Partner

“Relocation company” is not a protected professional title in the way that, say, a real estate broker licence is. Some providers are excellent; some are sales operations with a translator on call. Due diligence is not bureaucracy here — it is the only way to tell them apart.

A practical due-diligence checklist

Questions to put to every prospective partner
Area What to ask Why it matters
Relevant experience How many international employees have you supported in the last year, in which industries? Experience with students and experience with corporate transferees are different products.
Languages supported Which languages do your staff actually speak, and can we meet the case worker? A translated brochure is not the same as an interpreter at city hall.
Geographic coverage Which cities do you serve directly, and where do you use subcontractors? National claims often mean a partner network you have never met.
Pricing transparency Can we have an itemised written scope with taxes, optional extras and cancellation costs? A total without components cannot be compared or audited.
Service scope Which tasks are included, which are referred out, and who owns each handover? Most disputes arise at the boundary between two providers.
Response times What are your working hours, escalation route and expected response windows? Arrivals do not respect office hours; ask what actually happens at 8pm on a Sunday.
Data handling What personal data do you collect, why, who receives it and how long is it kept? Passport, residence card and My Number data need a secure channel and a clear purpose.
Contract terms What are the payment terms, notice period and liability for third-party vendors? You are buying an outcome, not a set of emails.
Complaints process Who handles a complaint, and what is the written procedure? A provider that cannot answer this is telling you something.
Registration and qualifications If you claim RSO or licensed-agent status, show the registration or licence number and where we can verify it. For RSOs use the official register; for agents use the takken search. Registration is a status check, not a quality rating.

Run a small test before a big commitment

For a first provider, scope one real case rather than signing an annual arrangement. Give both sides a written brief, define the output you expect, and hold a short review at the end. A provider that handles one hire well has earned a larger conversation; one that cannot explain a fee on a small job will not improve on a big one.

7. Common Mistakes Employers Should Avoid

  • Assuming all foreign employees have the same support obligations. SSW (i) has a specific ten-item regime; other statuses do not carry the same published plan.
  • Booking accommodation the employee cannot register as an address. Check before paying, not after arrival.
  • Confusing a guarantor with a rent guarantee company. They are different products with different paperwork and risk.
  • Promising a bank account or a lease approval. Banks, landlords and guarantee companies make their own decisions.
  • Leaving the 14-day notification to “the employee, somehow”. Put the deadline in the schedule and offer accompaniment where appropriate.
  • Treating a shared inbox as support. Name a person for the first two weeks.
  • Buying a package you do not need. Map the tasks first, then decide which ones need outside help.
  • Forgetting the exit. When an assignment ends, someone still has to clear the apartment, handle the four appliance categories and close the utilities. Plan that at the start of the tenancy, not in the final week.
  • Confusing credentials. A relocation provider is not automatically a Registered Support Organization; a rent guarantee company is not a licensed real estate broker.

9. Working With Independent Service Providers

Even with a relocation partner, you will probably touch several specialists. The value comes from treating them as a coordinated set rather than a stack of invoices.

🏠 Housing and accommodation

Lease structure, guarantor arrangements, move-in condition and the address the employee can register. This is usually the first dependency to resolve.

💼 Moving and clearance

Unpacking at the start; at the end, furniture removal, appliance recycling and an apartment ready for inspection. Ask about the four appliance categories covered by Japan's recycling rules.

📱 Internet and mobile

A short-term connection for arrival week and a long-term plan once the employee has an address. Check device compatibility and cancellation terms.

📶 Translation and interpretation

Use a qualified interpreter for anything that affects a legal or financial decision. A bilingual colleague is helpful but may not be qualified for a contract or an office procedure.

🗺️ Local orientation

Transport, garbage rules, emergency numbers and where to get free official help. Much of this can be covered with official guides rather than a paid tour.

📦 Household setup

Bedding, basic appliances and a first-night kit. These are small costs that prevent a bad first impression.

Apply the same basic diligence to each: legal identity, written scope, itemised pricing, cancellation terms, data handling and a complaints route. A small provider with a clear written process is often easier to work with than a large one with a polished brochure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an employer need to arrange before a foreign employee arrives in Japan? +

At minimum, confirm the written working conditions, the employee's status of residence and work authorisation, where they will live on arrival, who will meet them, and how they will reach the office. If the employee is a Specified Skilled Worker (i), a support plan is also required as part of the residence application. Beyond that, useful preparation includes a first-week schedule, a Japanese-language point of contact, and clear written instructions for the municipal procedures the employee must complete.

Is an employer required to find housing for foreign employees in Japan? +

There is no single rule that applies to every hire. For Specified Skilled Worker (i), the official ten support items include helping the worker secure housing and assisting with contracts for utilities, a bank account and a mobile phone, which may include acting as a guarantor or providing company housing. For other residence statuses, the official pages do not list the same support plan. Employment contracts, collective agreements and the MHLW guideline on employment management still matter, so check what applies to your own hire.

What support is mandatory under Specified Skilled Worker (i)? +

The Immigration Services Agency lists ten forms of support that companies are required to provide to Specified Skilled Worker (i) employees: pre-arrival guidance; airport pick-up and drop-off; housing and contract assistance; a life-orientation session; accompanying the worker to public procedures; Japanese-language learning opportunities; consultation and complaint handling; opportunities to interact with Japanese people; job-change support if the company intends to lay the worker off; and regular meetings at least once every three months. See the official ISA page.

Can an employer outsource SSW (i) support to a Registered Support Organization? +

Yes. The Immigration Services Agency says an accepting organisation may entrust all or part of the support plan to a Registered Support Organization, and that an organisation entrusted with the whole plan is treated as meeting its support-structure standard. An RSO may not sub-entrust the work. Check the current official register before appointing one.

Do engineers and highly skilled professionals need the same support plan? +

The official ten-item support regime is expressly tied to Specified Skilled Worker (i). The Immigration Services Agency pages for Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services and Highly Skilled Professional do not list an equivalent mandatory support plan. Practical onboarding help can still be valuable, but it should not be described as the same statutory duty. Confirm the current rules for the status you are using.

How soon must a foreign employee register an address in Japan? +

The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications states that medium- to long-term residents must submit a move-in notification to the municipality of their residence within fourteen days of taking up their new residence, bringing their Residence Card. Employers should build this into the first-week schedule but leave the employee or a qualified adviser to handle the procedure. See the MIC guidance.

What should HR check before hiring a relocation company in Japan? +

Check the legal company name and address, relevant licences or registrations where applicable, actual language coverage, the areas they serve, a written scope of work, itemised fees, cancellation terms, data handling, a complaints route and who will handle your account. Registration or licensing confirms status, not quality, so ask for references you can verify and keep the agreement in writing.

Does an employer have to pay a guarantor or guarantee company for an employee's apartment? +

Not under a universal rule. In the rental market, a guarantor or a rent guarantee company is commonly required by the landlord or agent. Under Specified Skilled Worker (i), assisting with housing and contracts is one of the official support items, and acting as a guarantor or providing company housing is one way to meet it. Who pays depends on the employment terms and the specific support plan, so agree it in writing before the employee arrives.

What is the difference between a relocation company and a Registered Support Organization? +

A relocation company is a commercial service provider that may help with moving, housing, admin or orientation. A Registered Support Organization is an organisation registered with the Immigration Services Agency to carry out Specified Skilled Worker (i) support duties on behalf of an accepting organisation. The two roles can overlap in practice, but they are not the same credential, and a relocation company should not be described as an RSO unless it appears on the official register.

Conclusion and Next Steps

Hiring someone from overseas is an operational project as much as a recruitment one. The organisations that handle it well are rarely the ones with the most elaborate policy documents. They are the ones that decide early who owns each dependency, keep the legal distinction between SSW (i) support and general onboarding clear, and write the handover down.

  1. Write down the status and the support duty. If it is SSW (i), start with the official ten-item list and the support plan.
  2. Choose a housing approach and an arrival address. Then confirm the address works for registration.
  3. Name one person and issue a one-page first-week sheet. Include the 14-day notification deadline and an emergency contact.
  4. Map tasks before buying services. Outsource the run, keep the decisions.
  5. Plan the exit at the start. The end of a tenancy is a predictable event; treat it as one.

If you are an employer, you can discuss a clearance need or browse the other guides. If you are a provider, start with the partners page and send a short introduction.
