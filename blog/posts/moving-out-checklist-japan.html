The Complete Moving Out Checklist in Japan for Foreigners

Published October 9, 2026  |  Moving Out  |  12 min read

Leaving a rented apartment in Japan is a chain of small deadlines, and each one sits with a different party — your landlord, your utility providers, your municipality and you. Miss one and it usually costs money or time. This checklist walks through the whole sequence in order, from reading the contract to handing back the keys, so you can work through it once and tick it off.

⚠️ Read this first: procedures, deadlines, fees and waste-disposal rules in Japan vary by municipality, by utility or internet provider, and by your individual rental contract. Nothing on this page overrides any of those three. Treat the timeframes below as a planning guide and confirm the exact requirement that applies to your address, your providers and your agreement.

A Realistic Timeline

The exact spacing depends on your contract and your municipality, but most moves follow the same order. Work backwards from your move-out date:

1

6–8 Weeks Before

Read your contract. Confirm the notice period and the notice format. Book your movers. Check your municipality's oversized waste reservation lead time.

2

4 Weeks Before

Send written notice to your landlord or management company. Arrange utility closures and transfers. Start sorting what you are taking, selling, donating or disposing of.

3

2 Weeks Before

Confirm internet cancellation or transfer. File your address change. Book oversized waste collection. Deep-clean as you empty each room rather than leaving it all to the end.

4

Final Week

Confirm the inspection appointment in writing. Photograph every empty room. Settle outstanding bills. Re-confirm the closing readings for electricity, gas and water.

5

Move-Out Day

Walk the apartment with the landlord or agent, photograph the condition, hand over every key and remote, and get written confirmation that the unit was returned.

6

After You Leave

Make sure the landlord has a forwarding address for the deposit settlement. Keep your contract, inspection record and receipts until the settlement is final.

1. Review Your Rental Contract

Before you notify anyone, find your rental contract and the important-terms explanation you received when you signed. You will refer to these twice: once to find the notice requirement, and again when the deposit is settled. Keep both documents until the settlement is finished — losing them makes any dispute much harder to argue.

Look for these specific clauses:

  • Notice period and notice format — how far in advance you must notify, and whether the notice must be in writing, on a specific form, or delivered in a particular way.
  • Move-out date — whether the contract ends on a fixed date or on the date you give notice for.
  • Restoration obligations — what condition the unit must be returned in, and what falls outside normal wear and tear.
  • Special clauses — cleaning charges, key replacement, carpet or curtain replacement, and any fixed fee deducted at move-out. These are contract-specific and can materially change your final cost.
  • Deposit settlement terms — the timing and the process for returning your deposit.
🔍 Official guidance: the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Housing publishes guidance on moving in and moving out of rented housing, covering contract review, restoration, and confirming the notice requirements before you sign anything. It is published in Japanese.

2. Notify Your Landlord or Management Company

Once you know the notice period, notify the party named in your contract — that is often the management company rather than the landlord directly. Send it in the format your contract requires and keep proof of delivery. A phone call is useful for speed, but a written record is what protects you if the date is later disputed.

Ask for written confirmation of:

  • The move-out date your notice has been accepted for.
  • The date and time of the final inspection, and who will attend.
  • What you are expected to have done before the inspection — cleaning, rubbish removal, utility closures, key return.
  • Where to send your forwarding address for the deposit settlement.

If your contract sets a notice period of about one month, that is common, but it is not universal — some contracts require more, and some arrangements differ for fixed-term tenancies. Your contract is the authority here, not a general rule of thumb.

For a worked example of what tenants are typically advised to check at move-out, the National Consumers Affairs Center of Japan publishes guidance on handling rental move-out disputes, including what to confirm before you sign anything.

3. Arrange Utilities (Electricity, Gas, Water)

Electricity, gas and water are three separate arrangements, and each is cancelled or transferred with its own provider on its own paperwork. Contact each one individually — closing one does not close the others.

Electricity

Contact your electricity supplier to request a closing reading and account closure. Schedule the reading for a specific date around your move, and note that the account holder must match the person who signed the contract. If you are moving within Japan and staying with the same retailer, ask whether a transfer is cheaper than a cancellation plus a new connection.

Gas

Gas requires a closing reading, and many providers require the account holder to be present for the final visit or to authorise someone in writing. Book this early — appointment slots fill up around peak moving season.

Water

Water is generally administered by your municipality or a contracted office rather than a retail supplier. Submit the closure request with the date you want it to take effect and settle any outstanding balance.

Providers publish their own cancellation terms, notice periods and closing fees, and those differ between companies, plans and regions. Check the current terms on your own provider's site rather than relying on a figure quoted anywhere else. As starting points:

  • TEPCO (electricity, Kanto region)
  • Tokyo Gas (gas, Tokyo and surrounding areas)
  • Your municipality's water office — search for your city or ward name plus 水道 届出 (water notification).

4. Internet and Address Changes

Internet and phone

Broadband cancellation is the step most often left too late. Depending on your line and contract, you may be able to transfer your existing contract to a new address instead of cancelling and reconnecting, which avoids a new installation wait. Ask your provider which applies before you give notice either way.

Cancellation notice periods for fixed broadband are commonly longer than a month and can be longer still depending on the line, the plan and whether you are still within a minimum term. This is provider-specific — check your own contract. Major providers and their portals:

  • flets — NTT broadband service portal
  • NTT East and NTT West — line providers

If you hold a mobile number you want to keep, check separately whether your number can be retained through the change — that is a different process from the address itself.

Address change

At your municipal or ward office, file the moving-out notice (転出届, tenshutsu-todoke). The deadline, the documents required and the office that handles it differ between municipalities, so confirm the current requirements with your own city or ward office before you go. The same applies to any identity card, residence record or professional registration that carries your address — check what must be updated for your particular situation.

Official starting points:

  • Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications — administration of the resident records system
  • Digital Agency — MyNumber and related administrative services

Mail: arrange mail redirection through Japan Post. Bills, bank letters and government documents will otherwise continue arriving at the old address, which is exactly the mail you cannot afford to miss during a deposit settlement.

5. Sort Belongings and Oversized Waste

Start this earlier than feels necessary. Reservation slots for oversized waste collection can be booked out well in advance, and disposal services around the end of the month are busy.

Sort everything into four piles: take with you, sell, donate or pass on, and dispose of. For the last pile, confirm how your municipality handles each type of item before you assume anything.

  • Oversized items generally require an advance reservation and a disposal sticker through your municipality's oversized waste (粗大ゴミ, sodai gomi) system. Lead times and prices are set locally.
  • Designated home appliances fall under Japan's designated recycling scheme and usually cannot go out with ordinary oversized waste. The customer who discards them is normally responsible for the recycling fee, so budget for this.
  • Everything left in the unit is likely to be removed by the landlord and charged against your deposit, frequently at a higher cost than arranging it yourself.

The Ministry of the Environment publishes waste and recycling information, but collection itself is organised municipality by municipality — always confirm the rule for your own address.

For a detailed walkthrough of the disposal options, costs and legal requirements, see our guide to disposing of furniture in Japan.

6. Clean the Apartment

Clean as you empty each room rather than leaving the whole apartment for the final days. Two practical reasons: you can see what you are actually cleaning once the furniture is gone, and you get a second pass if something needs more attention.

Focus on the areas that most often generate a charge:

  • Kitchen — cooker top, extractor fan, inside the cupboards and any grease on surfaces.
  • Bathroom and toilet — limescale, drain areas, and any mould around seals.
  • Walls and flooring — scuffs, marks, and any damage that is genuinely yours to fix.
  • Balcony — dirt, leaves, and anything stored that will count as leftover property.
  • Windows, fittings and light fixtures.

Photograph each room once it is clean and empty, with the date visible if you can manage it. These photos are the counterweight to any claim made later about the condition you left behind.

Whether cleaning charges are valid at all depends on what your contract says. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Housing maintains a Q&A on restoration disputes, including how wear and tear is generally distinguished from damage the tenant must pay for.

7. Attend the Final Inspection

Make every effort to attend in person. The inspection is your only real opportunity to see the apartment's condition assessed while you are still there to respond to it.

During the walkthrough:

  • Go room by room with the landlord or agent, and photograph anything they point out.
  • Compare against your own move-in record and your move-out photos.
  • Ask for the itemised basis of any charge mentioned, and whether it is covered by a special clause in your contract.
  • Confirm the keys, remotes and access cards being handed over.
  • Do not sign anything you have not read and understood. Ask for a copy of whatever you sign.
📝 Useful tool: the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Housing publishes an example condition and restoration checklist (PDF, Japanese) that both parties can fill in during the move-in and move-out walkthrough. Bringing something equivalent of your own has the same effect.

If a charge is disputed after the fact, you are not without options. The National Consumers Affairs Center of Japan publishes statistics and commentary on rental restoration disputes, and you can reach a local consultation desk through the consumer hotline 188 (iya-ya). The Japan Legal Support Center (Houterasu) offers legal consultation, and the Consumer Affairs Agency publishes consumer guidance.

8. Return the Keys

Hand back every physical item your contract lists: all keys, entry phones, remote controls, access cards, parking remotes, and any storage or mailbox keys. Missing items are usually charged for at replacement cost, and that is one of the easiest charges to avoid.

Before you leave, make sure you have:

  • Written confirmation that the unit was returned, with the date and the name of the person who accepted it.
  • The forwarding address lodged with the landlord or management company, so the deposit settlement can be sent to you.
  • Your contract, move-out photos, inspection record and any receipts, kept together until the settlement is complete.

Deposit settlement is not instantaneous, and it can arrive after you have left the country. Having the paperwork in one place means you can respond without needing to retrieve anything from the old apartment.

Why the Rules Vary — and How to Confirm Yours

This is the part that catches people out. Three different bodies control three different parts of your move-out, and none of them is bound by the others:

Your Municipality

Runs waste collection and the oversized waste system, handles the moving-out notice, and administers water in many areas. Rules and lead times are set locally and differ between neighbouring cities.

Your Providers

Electricity, gas, water and internet each set their own notice periods, closing fees, paperwork and appointment availability — and they differ by company, plan and region.

Your Contract

Sets the notice period, restoration obligations, special cleaning clauses and deposit settlement terms. This document governs your relationship with the landlord, whatever general practice says.

The practical habit that prevents most problems: before acting on anything you read online — including this page — check it against your own address, your own providers and your own contract. If those three sources disagree with each other, the contract usually settles the landlord question, your municipality settles the waste and administrative questions, and your provider settles the rest.

Where you need a second opinion, the National Consumers Affairs Center of Japan and your local consumer affairs desk are free to contact.

Frequently Asked Questions About Moving Out in Japan

How much notice do I have to give before moving out? +

It is set by your rental contract, not by a single national rule. Many contracts ask for notice about one month in advance, but the exact period, the form the notice must take, and any exceptions are written in your agreement. Read your contract and confirm the period in writing with your landlord or management company.

Do I have to be present for the final inspection? +

You should try to attend. An in-person walkthrough lets you photograph the condition of the apartment together with the landlord or agent, record anything already damaged or worn, and ask about any charge before you sign. If you cannot attend, ask whether someone you authorise may attend on your behalf.

Who do I contact to cancel electricity, gas and water? +

Each provider separately. Electricity and gas suppliers take a closing reading and a cancellation request, water is usually handled by your municipality or its contracted office, and internet is cancelled or transferred through your broadband provider. Each has its own notice period, closing fee and paperwork, so check each contract individually.

Can I leave furniture in the apartment when I move out? +

In most cases no. Rental contracts commonly require the unit to be emptied, and anything left behind is usually cleared by the landlord and charged against your deposit. Oversized items normally need an advance reservation through your municipality's oversized waste system, and some appliances must go to a designated recycling scheme. Rules differ by city, so confirm with your municipality.

Why do moving-out rules differ so much between cities? +

Because responsibility is split. Waste collection is run municipality by municipality, utility and internet terms are set by each provider and plan, and notice periods, cleaning obligations and restoration charges come from your individual rental contract. Always confirm the rule that applies to your address, your provider and your agreement.
