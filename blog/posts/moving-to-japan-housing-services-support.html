Moving to Japan in 2026: How to Find Housing, Essential Services, and Reliable Local Support

Published November 15, 2026  |  Moving In  |  22 min read

Moving to Japan is rarely one decision. It is a chain of small dependencies: an apartment needs a phone number, a phone contract often needs an address, the address needs a municipal notification, and the notification is easier once you have somewhere to live. This guide puts that chain in a practical order, separates official requirements from editorial advice, and shows you how to check the businesses that sit around each step.

The Short Answer

If you only remember five things, remember these:

  • Housing: Start with the transport ministry's free Apartment Search Guidebook. Expect a guarantor or a rental guarantee company, an itemised upfront cost, a screening period, and a contract explanation before you sign.
  • Connectivity: Compare a fixed fibre line, a home router, pocket Wi-Fi and a mobile SIM or eSIM by contract length, installation, data limits, cancellation and the payment method the provider accepts. Do not assume an imported phone will work on every network.
  • Paperwork: Submit your move-in notification at the municipal office within 14 days of taking up residence, and ask about National Health Insurance and pension at the same visit. The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare publish the official timing.
  • Providers: Verify the legal identity and any required licence, get the scope and fees in writing, read the cancellation terms, and keep a complaints route. Registration is a check, not a quality guarantee.
  • What not to do: Do not send passport copies, My Number details or large payments to an individual you cannot verify, and do not treat a forum post as legal or immigration advice.
⚖️ Scope note: This is practical information, not legal, immigration, tax or financial advice. Rules, fees and provider policies change. Confirm anything that affects your status of residence, employment, taxes or a signed contract with the issuing authority or a qualified professional.

1. Before You Move

The most useful preparation is not a longer packing list. It is understanding the order in which things can be done. A Japanese address unlocks municipal paperwork. Municipal paperwork unlocks some phone and bank applications. A phone number unlocks apartment screening and delivery services. Fixed internet may need landlord permission and an installation appointment. Trying to solve all of that on arrival day is how a manageable move becomes a stressful one.

Documents to keep accessible

Requirements vary by procedure and by municipality, but these are the documents you should not bury in checked luggage:

  • passport and visa or Certificate of Eligibility, if applicable;
  • residence card, issued to medium- to long-term residents on arrival in most cases;
  • My Number card or notification card, once issued;
  • employment contract, offer letter, student enrolment documents or other proof of your activity in Japan;
  • recent proof of income or bank statements, if an agent or guarantee company asks;
  • guarantor information, if you have a proposed joint guarantor;
  • a personal seal (hanko or inkan), if a procedure you are using requires one;
  • passport-style photographs, which some applications still request;
  • for family members joining a household, documents proving the relationship, with Japanese translations where required.

The relationship-document point is not optional trivia. The MIC's English guidance on moving in and out specifically notes that family members may need birth or marriage certificates issued by public agencies in their home country, together with Japanese translations.

A realistic sequence

A practical moving timeline (editorial recommendation, not an official rule)
When What to do Why it matters
8 to 12 weeks before Research areas, commute, rent range, ward office, and whether your workplace or school offers housing support. You cannot judge a listing without knowing the daily commute and the total monthly cost.
4 to 8 weeks before Shortlist agents or housing providers, prepare documents, and ask what screening requires for your nationality and status. Screening is not automatic; requirements differ by landlord and guarantee company.
2 to 4 weeks before Apply, attend a viewing or video viewing, review the important-points explanation, and confirm the itemised move-in cost. This is where deposits, key money, agency fees and guarantee fees become concrete.
1 to 2 weeks before Arrange utilities, internet installation, moving access, and a temporary connection for your phone. Some installations need landlord permission or a building visit; last-minute requests can slip.
Arrival week Submit the move-in notification, ask about health insurance and pension, and update your address with service providers. The 14-day clock starts when you take up residence, not when you feel settled.
First month Set up garbage rules, a local payment method, any available municipal services, and a simple household budget. Small administrative gaps become expensive when a bill or notice goes to the wrong address.

What to verify before you pay anyone

  • Who is the landlord, agent and, if applicable, guarantee company?
  • Is the agent a licensed real estate transaction business, and does the licence number appear on the documents?
  • Is the total move-in cost itemised in writing, including tax and any optional product?
  • Is the contract an ordinary lease or a fixed-term lease, and what happens at renewal?
  • What notice period applies if you leave early, and what restoration costs are possible?
  • Which payment methods are accepted, and will you receive a receipt?

If an individual asks for a large cash payment before any contract, refuses to identify the landlord or agency, or pressures you to decide before you have read the documents, stop. A legitimate process can survive a request for written details. A process that cannot is not one you should rush.

2. Finding Accommodation

Japan's rental market is not difficult because listings are secret. It is difficult because the process combines screening, a guarantor system, unfamiliar upfront costs and a contract explanation that matters. Once you know the sequence, it becomes much less mysterious.

The rental process, step by step

The following sequence is based on the transport ministry's Apartment Search Guidebook, which is published in English and 13 other languages:

  1. Tell the agent your requirements. Station, rent ceiling, size, move-in date, language support and any must-have facilities.
  2. View the property. The guidebook notes that viewing is free and you are not obliged to apply if you are not satisfied.
  3. Submit a tenant application. Expect questions about your name, occupation, co-occupants, income, Japanese ability and guarantor situation.
  4. Go through screening. The agent and landlord conduct a tenancy examination. The guidebook says it usually takes one to two weeks from application to move-in.
  5. Receive the important-points explanation. Before signing, the agent should explain the contract and the property, including the type of lease, deposit, key money, restoration obligations and cancellation terms.
  6. Sign and pay. The guidebook lists the security deposit, key money, agency fee, damage insurance premium, first month's rent and common service fee as typical payments.
  7. Receive the keys and move in. Confirm utility contacts, garbage rules and the building's move-in rules before the day.
🏦 Official guidance: The guidebook states that when forming a rental agreement in Japan, use of a rental guarantee company or providing a guarantor is necessary in case the tenant cannot pay the rent. Ask the real estate agent which option the landlord accepts. A guarantee company is not the same thing as a joint guarantor, and the landlord may require one, the other, or both.

Guarantor companies and joint guarantors

A rental guarantee company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha) screens you and agrees to cover unpaid rent under its contract with the landlord. A joint guarantor (連帯保証人, rentai hoshōnin) is usually a person who accepts a legal obligation alongside you. The two are different, and the paperwork, fees and risk are different too.

The transport ministry has operated a registration system for rent guarantee companies that meet certain requirements since October 2017. It also publishes a list of registered rent guarantee companies that offer multilingual support. That list is a research starting point, not a recommendation: companies set their own screening, fees and renewal terms.

If you do not have a suitable guarantor in Japan, say so early. Some agents work regularly with guarantee companies that accept foreign residents; others do not. Waiting until after you have paid an application fee is the wrong time to discover the mismatch.

What you actually pay upfront

The guidebook gives a rule of thumb of four to seven months' rent for the payments required at contract signing. That sounds alarming until you see the components. It is also a rule of thumb, not a quote for your apartment.

Common initial rental payments in Japan
Item What it is What to confirm
Security deposit (敷金, shikikin) Money held against unpaid rent or damage; the guidebook describes it as usually about two months' rent. How much is refundable, what deductions are allowed, and when the settlement is paid.
Key money (礼金, reikin) A non-refundable payment to the landlord in some listings. Whether it is required, how many months, and whether it is negotiable.
Agency fee (仲介手数料, chūkai tesūryō) The agent's fee for acting as intermediary. The rate, the tax, and exactly what service it covers.
Guarantee company fee A fee for the guarantee contract; the guidebook notes it is often about half a month's rent for a two-year guarantee. Initial fee, annual or renewal fee, and what the company will and will not cover.
Damage insurance Insurance required by many landlords. Coverage, exclusions, policy term and whether you may choose your own insurer.
First rent and common service fee Rent is commonly paid one month in advance, plus building common fees. Due date, payment method, and whether the common fee changes.

Ask for the full list before you apply, not after approval. A written breakdown makes it easier to compare two apartments and much easier to spot a charge that was never explained.

Read the contract, not just the listing

Before signing, you should receive an important-points explanation (重要事項説明, jūyō jikō setsumei). Pay attention to:

  • whether the lease is an ordinary rental agreement or a fixed-term lease with no automatic renewal;
  • the renewal fee and renewal period, if any;
  • the notice period for termination, which the guidebook says is usually one to two months and should be given in writing;
  • restoration obligations and any non-refundable cleaning or restoration fee;
  • rules on subletting, pets, instruments, smoking and guests;
  • who handles repairs and emergencies, and how to contact them;
  • the exact bank account and payment schedule for rent.

If you are leaving a Japanese apartment later, our guide to deposit deductions explains normal wear, special clauses and itemised statements in more detail. It is written for the exit side, but reading it before you sign also helps you understand what the entry inspection is protecting.

Housing options you will encounter

Common housing categories (editorial comparison, not an availability list)
Option Usually best for Check before committing
Standard rental apartment (賃貸, chintai) People staying at least a year who want their own lease and address. Screening, guarantor, upfront cost, contract type, restoration and notice period.
Share house Short first stays, lower initial cost and a built-in social environment. House rules, shared facilities, deposit and cancellation terms, and whether the operator is properly identified.
Monthly apartment (マンスリーマンション) Assignments or transitions where a furnished short stay is useful. Minimum stay, utilities, cleaning fee, extension terms and what is actually included.
Company or school housing People whose employer or school provides accommodation support. Who signs the lease, what happens if your contract ends, and which rules you must follow.
Temporary hotel or serviced stay The first days while you view properties or wait for screening. Storage, mail handling, address registration options and the cost of staying longer than planned.

None of these categories is inherently better. The right choice depends on how long you will stay, how much cash you can commit upfront, whether you need a registered address immediately, and how much uncertainty you can absorb while screening finishes.

Questions to ask before you apply

  • What is the total move-in cost, itemised, including tax?
  • Which guarantee company is used, and what are its initial and renewal fees?
  • Is a joint guarantor required in addition to the guarantee company?
  • Is the contract ordinary or fixed-term, and what is the renewal process?
  • How much notice must I give before leaving, and in what form?
  • What restoration or cleaning charges are specified, and what is normal wear?
  • Is the building fibre-ready, and does installation need landlord permission?
  • Which utilities and internet providers serve the building?
  • What are the garbage rules, collection days and bulky-waste procedure?
  • Are there fees for parking, bicycles, communal areas or a management association?
  • What are the rules on noise, pets, instruments and guests?
  • Who is the emergency contact, and is support available in a language you understand?

Check the agent's licence

Real estate agents in Japan must be licensed as a real estate transaction business (宅地建物取引業, takken-gyō). The transport ministry operates a public search system for licensed businesses (Japanese interface). The licence number should also appear on the documents you receive. This check does not tell you whether an agent is good, but it does tell you whether the business is licensed to act in the transaction.

🚨 Red flags in a rental process: no verifiable agency or landlord; a demand for cash with no receipt; a refusal to provide a written cost breakdown; pressure to sign before the important-points explanation; a contract that differs from what was explained; or a request to send identity documents through an unsecured chat before any agreement exists.

3. Setting Up Internet and Mobile Services

Connectivity is the second dependency. You need a phone number for apartment screening, delivery services and many accounts. You need internet at home for work, school and everyday administration. The mistake is assuming one product solves both, or that the cheapest headline price is the lowest total cost.

The main options

Internet and mobile options in Japan (editorial comparison)
Option How it works Check before signing
Fibre line (光回線, hikari kaisen) A fixed connection installed in the building or unit, usually with a router. Building availability, installation permission, construction work, contract length, cancellation fee and actual in-home speed.
Home router (ホームルーター) A plug-in mobile router for the home, without a fibre installation. Coverage at your address, data cap, throttling, contract term and whether the router is rented or bought.
Pocket Wi-Fi A portable battery-powered router shared between devices. Battery life, data limit, replacement policy, return method and cost if the device is lost.
Mobile SIM or eSIM A voice and data plan for your phone, with a physical SIM or a downloaded eSIM. Device compatibility, network bands, contract length, data cap, tethering, cancellation and identity documents.

For a first week, a short-term eSIM or rental pocket Wi-Fi can be useful because it does not depend on a home installation. For a long-term stay, most people end up comparing a mobile plan plus either fibre or a home router. The right combination depends on whether you work from home, how much data you use, and whether your building is already wired.

The address and payment problem

Long-term contracts usually require a Japanese address and a payment method the provider accepts. That can be a Japanese bank account, a credit card issued in Japan, or another method the provider lists. There is no single nationwide rule, and providers change their requirements. Ask before you leave your home country, and do not cancel your existing number until the new service is active.

If you are staying in temporary accommodation, ask the provider whether that address can be used for the contract, or whether you should wait until you have a permanent address. A short-term eSIM can keep you reachable in the meantime, but it may not support the identity verification or payment method that a long-term contract requires.

SIM locks and device compatibility

Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications publishes a mobile phone portal and SIM-lock guidelines. Under the current guidelines, handsets released in Japan on or after 1 October 2021 are in principle not SIM-locked, subject to limited exceptions. That is useful if you plan to bring a phone or buy a used one, but it does not guarantee that every imported device supports the bands, VoLTE or eSIM features a Japanese network uses.

Before you choose a plan, check the model number, supported bands, VoLTE support, eSIM support and whether the phone is carrier-locked to a foreign network. A phone that makes calls on one network may still lack the band coverage you need at home or at work.

Contract checks that matter more than the headline price

  • Contract length and cancellation: Is there a minimum term, an early-termination fee, or a device instalment that continues after cancellation?
  • Data cap and throttling: What happens after the cap, and is the cap shared across devices?
  • Tethering: Is hotspot use included, or does it cost extra?
  • Installation: Does fibre require drilling, a building visit or landlord approval? Who arranges it?
  • Coverage: Check the provider's coverage map for your exact address, not just the city.
  • Support language: Can you get help in English or another language you understand when the connection fails?
  • Equipment return: What must you return, by when, and what happens if it is damaged?
  • Payment: Which card or bank account is accepted, and what happens if a payment fails?

Keep the contract, the fee schedule and the cancellation instructions in one place. When you eventually move out, our moving-out checklist covers the internet, address-change and utility side of the exit in sequence.

4. Moving, Furniture and Household Essentials

Furnishing a Japanese home is a design problem as much as a shopping problem. Rooms can be smaller than you expect, doorways can be narrow, and some appliances carry disposal obligations when you leave. A little measuring before you buy saves a lot of lifting later.

Ship less than you think

International shipping is priced by volume and weight, and furniture is usually the worst value item to move. Before you ship a bed, sofa or appliance, compare the shipping cost with buying a suitable replacement in Japan. Check the voltage and plug type, and note that Japan's mains voltage is 100 V with different frequencies in eastern and western regions. An appliance designed for another voltage may need a transformer or may not be suitable at all.

Measure the doorway, stairwell, elevator and intended room before ordering anything large. Check whether the building requires a delivery reservation, protective sheeting or a specific time window. A sofa that fits the room but not the door is still a problem.

A first-night kit

Pack a small bag that does not depend on your shipment arriving:

  • bedding or a sleeping bag, and a towel;
  • phone charger, adaptor and a portable battery;
  • basic toiletries, toilet paper and hand soap;
  • a few days of medication in original packaging, with prescriptions if needed;
  • passport, residence card and key documents;
  • some cash for small purchases and transport;
  • a reusable water bottle and a simple breakfast;
  • the address of your accommodation and an offline map.

New, used or rented furniture

Japan has a large second-hand market, from physical chains to online marketplaces and community groups. Used furniture can be excellent value, but check operation, stains, odours, missing parts, delivery arrangements and whether the seller can provide a receipt. For appliances, ask for the model number and test it if possible. A warranty matters more than a low price if the item fails in the first month.

Rental furniture and appliance sets exist for short stays. Read the minimum term, monthly fee, delivery and collection charges, damage rules and what happens if you extend. The total cost over a year can be higher than buying, so compare the full term rather than the first month.

The four appliances you cannot put out as ordinary garbage

Japan's Home Appliance Recycling Law covers four categories of household appliance:

  • air conditioners;
  • televisions, including CRT, LCD, OLED and plasma types;
  • electric refrigerators and freezers;
  • electric washing machines and clothes dryers.

These items are not ordinary household waste. Retailers have take-back obligations and manufacturers have recycling obligations, and the consumer pays collection, transport and recycling fees. The Ministry of the Environment's overview (Japanese) explains the four categories and the cost split; the ministry's English waste and recycling portal is a useful starting point for the wider rules.

If you are buying a used appliance, ask how the previous owner would have disposed of it and what the recycling fee would be when you leave. That future cost belongs in your budget. Our guide to furniture disposal in Japan walks through the practical options, including retailer take-back, municipal bulky-waste collection and licensed clearance.

📏 Measure before you buy: room dimensions, door width, corridor turns, elevator depth and ceiling height. For appliances, check the power requirement, plug shape and whether the building has a dedicated outlet. For furniture, check whether it can be disassembled and whether the delivery company will carry it upstairs.

Building and neighbourhood rules

Ask the agent or building manager for the garbage schedule, the location of the collection point, the bulky-waste procedure and any rules about moving companies, elevator reservations or parking. Garbage rules are set locally; the transport ministry's guidebook notes that collection days and times differ by city, ward, town or village, and that improperly timed waste may not be collected even if it is sorted correctly.

5. Administrative and Everyday Support

The first two weeks after you take up residence are the most paperwork-heavy. The good news is that many procedures happen at the same municipal office, and some can be started online.

The first 14 days

Common administrative tasks after moving in
Task Where Timing Official source
Move-in notification (転入届, tennyūtodoke) Municipal office of your new address Within 14 days of taking up residence MIC move-in guidance
National Health Insurance (国民健康保険, kokumin kenkō hoken) Municipal insurance office Ask at the same visit; the health ministry advises completing the procedure within 14 days MHLW National Health Insurance guidance
Pension enquiries Municipal office or pension office; employer if you are enrolled at work At the same visit or as soon as your employment status is clear Japan Pension Service (English)

The health ministry's guidance also notes that you may not need to enrol in National Health Insurance in some cases. Ask the municipal office which category applies to you rather than assuming. The same principle applies to pension: your employer, status and income affect what you pay and whether an exemption or reduction is available.

Online moving procedures

Since 6 February 2023, all municipalities have been able to accept a move-out notification online through Mynaportal, together with a notification of when you plan to visit the new municipal office. The Digital Agency's online moving service page explains the flow.

You need a My Number Card with a valid electronic certificate for signature, or an eligible smartphone certificate. The online service can reduce a trip to the municipality you are leaving, but it does not eliminate the visit to the municipality you are joining: you still present your My Number Card at the counter to complete the move-in procedure. Private procedures such as electricity, gas and water may be available through private moving portals linked to the service.

If your card certificate is expired or your card is not ready, check the Digital Agency's list of cases where the online service cannot be used. Do not assume the online route is available just because you have a card.

Where to get free, official help

Two official resources are worth saving before you need them:

  • Foreign Residents Support Center (FRESC). A government contact point that supports the residence of foreign nationals. Houterasu, the Japan Legal Support Center, provides legal-system information in person and by phone at FRESC, free of charge and without a reservation. The English page lists a multilingual phone service in 10 languages and a walk-in desk on the 13th floor of Yotsuya Tower in Tokyo, open on weekdays from 9am to 5pm. This is legal information, not legal representation.
  • The Immigration Services Agency's living support portal. The portal brings together guidance for foreign residents, including daily-life and consultation information. It is a starting point for official information, not a substitute for your municipal office.

Local international associations and municipal counters can also help with everyday questions, but their services differ by area. Check your municipality's website rather than assuming a particular language or service is available.

⏰ Do not treat a deadline as a formality. The 14-day move-in notification is an official requirement, not a recommendation. If you are unsure which procedures apply to your status, ask the municipal office or a qualified adviser. This guide cannot tell you what your individual immigration or tax position requires.

6. Finding the Right Service Provider

Most people moving to Japan will deal with several providers: an agent, a moving company, a cleaner, an internet seller, perhaps an insurance or tax adviser. Some are excellent. A few are not. A short verification routine protects you without turning the process into a suspicion exercise.

A practical provider checklist
Check How to check Why it matters
Legal identity Ask for the company name, address and corporate number. Look it up in the National Tax Agency's corporate number database. A corporate number confirms that a corporation exists. It does not confirm quality, and sole proprietors may not appear.
Required licence For real estate, search the transport ministry's takken search and compare the licence number on the documents. Some activities require a licence. A licence is a minimum, not a recommendation.
Written scope Ask for a written description of what is included, what is excluded and who is responsible for each step. Most disputes start with an assumption about scope, not a hidden criminal intent.
Itemised fees Request a breakdown with tax, optional extras, cancellation fees and payment timing. A total without components cannot be compared or checked later.
Language support Ask who will handle your case and whether the contract and key documents are available in a language you understand. A salesperson who speaks English may not be the person handling the work or the complaint.
Terms and cancellation Read the cancellation, refund, rescheduling and no-show terms before paying. Cooling-off rights do not apply to every contract, and business-to-business deals are different.
Reviews Look for patterns across several sources, including dates and detailed complaints. Treat a single five-star review as one data point. Reviews can inform a decision but are not a licence, audit or guarantee.
Data handling Ask what personal data is collected, why, who receives it and how long it is kept. Send sensitive documents through a secure channel, not an open chat. Passport, residence card and My Number data deserve more protection than a restaurant booking.
Complaints route Ask for a named contact and a written complaints procedure before you need it. A provider that cannot explain its complaints route is telling you something.

A ten-minute verification routine

  1. Write down the legal company name, not just the brand name.
  2. Find the corporate number and check it in the NTA database.
  3. If the service is regulated, check the relevant licence or registration.
  4. Ask for the scope and fee breakdown by email, so there is a written record.
  5. Read the cancellation and complaints terms.
  6. Check that the bank account name matches the company or a clearly explained payment processor.
  7. Keep copies of the quote, contract, receipt and messages.

Cooling-off and consumer help

Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency publishes an English guide to the Specified Commercial Transactions Act and door-to-door sales. For door-to-door sales, a consumer may withdraw an offer or cancel a contract by writing to the business within eight days after receiving the statutory documents. The rule has conditions and exclusions. It is not a universal cancellation right for every service, and it does not apply to every business-to-business contract.

If you have a consumer problem, the Consumer Hotline 188 connects you to a local consumer affairs centre; the call is free but call charges apply. The National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan also publishes information for consumers. For legal information, FRESC is a useful starting point; for legal representation, you need a qualified lawyer.

🚫 Red flags: a provider that cannot give a legal name or address; a demand for payment to a personal account without explanation; a refusal to put fees or cancellation terms in writing; a claim of “official” status that cannot be verified; pressure to sign on the day; or an offer that depends on you recruiting other customers.

7. For Businesses Serving International Residents

People arriving in Japan are not a single market. A student on a tight budget, a engineer relocating with a family, a remote worker on a digital nomad route and a company transferring an employee all have different needs. The businesses that earn trust are usually the ones that make the next step obvious and the total cost visible.

What this audience is trying to work out

💰 What will this really cost?

Not the headline rent or monthly plan, but the deposit, key money, agency fee, guarantee fee, installation, delivery, cancellation and taxes. A clear total beats a low number with surprises.

💬 Who will actually help me?

An English sales page is not the same as English support after the contract. Say who handles enquiries, in which language, on which days and through which channel.

📄 What do I have to do, and when?

Documents, appointments, deadlines and dependencies. A short checklist is more useful than a long brochure.

🔒 What happens if something goes wrong?

A named complaints route, a refund or rescheduling policy, and a clear explanation of who is responsible for what.

What useful content looks like

  • It answers one real question. “What documents does a guarantor company ask for?” is more useful than “Everything you need to know about Japan.”
  • It cites a source. Official guidance, a published terms page or a dated provider document. If the answer depends on the reader's status or municipality, say so.
  • It sets limits. “We operate in these areas, for these customer types, with these response times” is more credible than “nationwide”.
  • It discloses commercial relationships. A paid placement, affiliate link or sponsorship should be clear where the reader sees it.
  • It is dated and maintained. Fees, rules and availability change. A page that was accurate two years ago may be misleading today.

What to avoid

  • invented reviews, ratings, awards or “official partner” claims;
  • guaranteed visa outcomes, apartment acceptance, deposit refunds or response times;
  • hidden fees, fake urgency or a price that only appears after you submit personal data;
  • claiming a government programme or another company's service is your own;
  • asking for passport, My Number or payment details through an insecure channel before there is a legitimate reason.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a guarantor to rent an apartment in Japan? +

The transport ministry's Apartment Search Guidebook says that forming a rental agreement in Japan requires either a rental guarantee company or a guarantor. In practice, the landlord and agent decide which they accept, so ask before you apply. A guarantee company screens you separately and charges a fee, usually about half a month's rent for a two-year guarantee according to the guidebook. See the English guidebook for the full explanation.

How much should I budget for initial rental costs in Japan? +

The transport ministry's guidebook gives a rule of thumb of four to seven months' rent at contract signing. That can include a security deposit, key money, agency fee, damage insurance, the first month's rent and common service fees. The exact total depends on the property, area and guarantee company, so ask for an itemised written breakdown before paying.

Can I get internet and a phone number before I have a Japanese address? +

A short-term eSIM or rental pocket Wi-Fi can bridge the gap before you have an address. A long-term mobile or fixed-internet contract usually requires a Japanese address and a payment method the provider accepts. Rules differ by provider, so confirm the documents and payment options before you rely on a connection for work or apartment screening.

What is the 14-day rule after moving to Japan? +

Foreign residents must submit a move-in notification to the municipality where they live within 14 days of taking up residence, bringing their residence card or passport. National Health Insurance procedures are also handled at the municipal office, and the health ministry advises completing them within 14 days. Ask the office which procedures apply to your situation. See the MIC guidance.

How can I check whether a Japanese service provider is legitimate? +

Check the company's corporate number in the National Tax Agency database, check a real estate agent's licence in the transport ministry's takken search, and ask for a written scope, itemised fees, cancellation terms and a complaints route. Registration confirms that a business exists and is licensed where required; it does not guarantee quality, so read the contract and keep copies.

No. Move Out Japan is an English-language move-out support service and guide, not a real estate agency, marketplace, booking platform or partner directory. We publish practical guides with official sources and quote for furniture and appliance clearance. We do not claim partnerships, listings, prices or availability that have not been agreed.

We accept partnership enquiries from businesses that serve people moving to, living in or leaving Japan. Start with the partners page and email a short introduction covering who you are, who you serve, where you operate, the model you propose and how you handle customer data and complaints. We do not promise traffic, leads, rankings, sales or commissions.

Does the 8-day cooling-off rule apply to every moving contract? +

No. The Consumer Affairs Agency explains that for door-to-door sales, a consumer may withdraw or cancel by writing within eight days after receiving the statutory documents. The rule has conditions and exclusions, and not every moving-related contract is a door-to-door sale. Keep the documents and ask the Consumer Hotline 188 or a consumer affairs centre if you are unsure.
