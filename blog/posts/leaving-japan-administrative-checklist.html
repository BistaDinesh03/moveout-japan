Moving Out of Japan: The Administrative Checklist Foreigners Often Miss

Published October 11, 2026  |  Moving Out  |  11 min read

Two situations look similar from the outside — you are leaving Japan for good, or you are moving to a different city inside it — and both trigger a set of administrative notifications that nothing automatically reminds you about. Get them wrong and the consequences follow you: mail that never arrives, a health insurance bill sent to your old address, a tax return you can no longer file from abroad, or a pension payment you can no longer claim. This checklist collects the official steps in the order that makes sense, with links to the government and public-body pages that publish them.

⚠️ Read this first: procedures, deadlines and eligibility rules in Japan vary by municipality, by your residence status and by your personal situation. Nothing on this page replaces advice from your city or ward office, the tax office, or the Japan Pension Service. Treat this as a planning guide and confirm the exact requirement that applies to you before you act — every section below links to the official source it came from.

Which Situation Are You In?

The paperwork splits into two paths, and most people are on one of them without realising both exist:

📍 Moving to another city in Japan

You file a moving-out notification (転出届, tenshutsu-todoke) at your current city or ward office — which also gets you the moving-out certificate you need later — and a moving-in notification (転入届, tennyu-todoke) at the new address. Your residence card is updated at both steps. Guidance published by the University of Osaka puts the moving-in notification at within 14 days of moving in when the move crosses city boundaries.

🛩 Leaving Japan entirely

On top of the municipal notification, you are dealing with mail forwarding that stops at Japan's border, health insurance settlement, a tax filing decision with a hard deadline, and a pension claim window that closes two years after you go. The official checklist for returning residents lists all of them as procedures to complete before departure.

The rest of this guide is ordered so that each step depends on the one before it: municipality first, because almost everything else asks for your registered address or a certificate from that office.

1. Tell Your Municipality First (転出 / 転入)

Your city or ward office is where the chain starts. Handle it first and the remaining steps become simpler; leave it to the end and you may find that other offices cannot process your request without the certificate this step produces.

Moving within the same city

Submit a change of address notification at the city office and have your new address written onto your residence card. The University of Osaka's guidance lists the residence card as the document to bring to the municipal or branch office.

Moving to a different city

Before you move out, go to your current city office, submit the moving-out notification, and have a moving-out certificate (転出証明書, tenshutsu-shoumeisho) issued. Then, within 14 days of moving in, take your residence card and that certificate to the city office at your new address and submit the moving-in notification. Without the certificate, the new office has no record of your move-out and the transfer cannot be completed properly.

Leaving Japan

The checklist the City of Sendai publishes for foreign residents lists a notification of moving out of Japan among the procedures to complete before departure, filed with the ward office or branch office where your residence is registered. The details — which counter, which documents, how early you can file — are set locally, so check with your own office rather than relying on another city's rules.

📝 Official guidance: the University of Osaka publishes Procedures at the City Office in English, covering moving-in and moving-out notifications, what to bring, and the 14-day window. The City of Sendai's Living Information pages for foreign residents publish the full return-home checklist this article draws on.

2. Forward Your Mail With Japan Post

Bank letters, tax notices, deposit settlements and utility refunds all arrive by post, which is exactly the mail you cannot afford to lose after you move. Japan Post's relocation service redirects post from your former address to your new one.

What its English service page actually says:

  • Free for one year. Items sent to the former address are redirected to the new address free of charge by submitting the relocation form at a post office counter near you.
  • The year runs from submission, not from the start date you request. Japan Post is explicit: the service "is effective for the period of one year from submitting the relocation form (not from the requested starting date of the forwarding service)".
  • After expiry, items go back to the sender. When the forwarding period ends, postal items are returned to the sender. To continue, you submit the relocation form again.
  • Bring two kinds of document. Identification for yourself (driver's licence, health insurance card and similar) plus something that proves your former address — passport, Individual Number Card, Basic Resident Register Card, residence certificate and similar documents issued by public bodies.
  • Do it before you move. Japan Post asks you to visit the counter and complete the procedure with enough time before your move date.
✉️ Mail cannot follow you overseas. Japan Post states directly in its guidance for international residents that it is unable to forward mail overseas. The one-year service is for a new address inside Japan. If you are leaving the country, give a Japanese address you trust (a friend, family member, or your management company) or ask the sender to stop paper mail entirely.

Registering online is possible, but note that Japan Post describes its website registration route as Japanese language only; going to the counter in person works regardless of your Japanese ability, and you can also hand in a filled-in relocation form directly. Official pages: Relocation/forwarding service and Japan Post's practical guide for international students.

3. Close or Move Your Health Insurance

If you are enrolled in National Health Insurance (国民健康保険, kokumin kensei hoken), the scheme expects you to settle up when you leave rather than simply stop paying.

The University of Osaka's guidance is specific about how: when you leave Japan, withdraw from the National Health Insurance, and once your return date is decided, take your insurance card, your flight ticket, and other documents showing your scheduled departure date to your municipal office. The office settles any surplus or shortfall in premiums and corrects the expiry date on your card to your departure date.

If you are moving between cities instead of leaving the country, your enrolment has to move with you. Ask the office at your old address and the office at your new one what each of them needs — the two procedures are separate, and neither city can do the other's step for you.

Health insurance appears as its own item on the official return-home checklist too, alongside pension and taxes: the City of Sendai lists health insurance among the procedures for returning home, so it is not an optional cleanup step.

4. Tax: Agent Notification or Final Return

This is the step that most often gets left until the airport. The National Tax Agency publishes two pages covering exactly what to do when you leave, and they agree with each other: you either hand the job to someone in Japan, or you finish it before you go.

Option A — appoint a tax agent

If you leave Japan, lose your domicile and residence for tax purposes, but are still subject to tax-return procedures, the NTA says you need to appoint a tax agent (納税管理人, nouzei kanrinin) who resides in Japan and submit the "Notification of Tax Agent for income tax / consumption tax" to the district director of the tax office with jurisdiction over your place for tax payment. The agent then handles those procedures on your behalf. The NTA states you may appoint either a Japanese corporation or a person who resides in Japan.

With that notification filed before departure, you file your return for the year through the agent between February 16 and March 15 of the following year, according to the agency's guidance.

Option B — finish it before you leave

Leave Japan without submitting the tax agent notification and the NTA's instruction is direct: you must file a quasi-final return (準確定申告, jun-kakutei shinkoku) and pay the tax before departure. Its separate page for departing residents repeats the same structure — final return and payment before departure if you have no agent, by the statutory due date if you do, and a quasi-final return before departure for certain non-residents whose employment income in Japan was not subject to withholding tax.

The agency also warns that individuals who have not paid their tax or filed a return by the specified due date may be imposed additional tax and delinquent tax. If you are unsure which category you fall into, that is a question for the tax office or a qualified adviser before your last week in the country — not something to guess at.

💰 Official sources: National Tax Agency, No.12004 — Income tax information for an individual who will leave Japan (tax agent and quasi-final return) and No.12021 — Procedures before departing from Japan. Both pages are published in English by the National Tax Agency.

5. Pension: The Two-Year Window

If you have paid into the Japanese pension system for a short period and are leaving the country, there is a claim you can make — but only inside a fixed window.

The Japan Pension Service states it plainly: if you are non-Japanese and leave Japan after short coverage periods under National Pension or Employees' Pension Insurance, you can apply for a lump-sum withdrawal payment (脱退一時金, dattai ichijikin). Its conditions are equally specific, and both matter:

  • You must make sure you are no longer covered by the National Pension or Employees' Pension Insurance schemes, and
  • You must file the application within two years since you leave Japan.

Miss that deadline and the claim is gone — there is no late application route published for it. The service page links to the application form and translations in several languages, so you can start the paperwork before your departure date rather than chasing it afterwards. Source: Japan Pension Service, Lump-sum Withdrawal Payments.

6. The Rest of the Official Checklist

The Sendai checklist is useful precisely because it is unglamorous: it lists the items that quietly fall off a foreign resident's radar while everyone focuses on flights and movers. Beyond the sections above, it flags:

  • Seal registration card — hand it in if you have one, since it stops being valid once your residence record goes.
  • Taxes beyond income tax — the checklist separates inhabitant tax and fixed asset / city planning tax as items to confirm, not just income tax.
  • Children's allowances — notification for child allowance, medical expense subsidies and single-parent support.
  • Schools — a notification to your child's school.
  • Cars and motorcycles — procedures for returning or disposing of vehicles, which is a separate process with its own deadlines.
  • Rent and utilities — final settlements, so nothing is collected after you have gone.
  • Senior cards and other municipal cards — anything issued under your resident record.

One more document worth handling carefully: your My Number paperwork. The University of Osaka's guidance notes that your Individual Number Notification Letter is mailed to your registered address and asks you to keep it safe even after returning to your home country — it is personal information, so treat it like you would a passport copy and never share the number itself casually.

A Sensible Order of Work

The deadlines do not all fall on the same day, and a few of them only start running once an earlier step is done. Work through it in this order:

1

Before you move

File the moving-out notification (or the notification of moving out of Japan) and get the moving-out certificate. Submit the Japan Post relocation form at the counter. Settle National Health Insurance with your departure documents.

2

Before departure

Either file the Notification of Tax Agent so your agent can act for you, or file the final or quasi-final return and pay before you leave. Nothing else substitutes for this step.

3

Within 14 days of moving in

For moves inside Japan: take your residence card and moving-out certificate to the new city office and file the moving-in notification. Confirm your health insurance transfer at the same time.

4

Within two years of leaving

File the lump-sum withdrawal payment application if you are eligible. Diarise this the day you depart — it is the only deadline in the list that expires silently.

5

As post arrives

Watch the Japanese address you nominated for forwarding. Deposit settlements, tax refunds and utility credits all arrive by mail, and forwarding stops after one year.

6

While boxes are still around

Clear the apartment itself. Furniture and appliances cannot go out with ordinary rubbish — oversized waste needs a reservation, and designated appliances carry a recycling process.

Frequently Asked Questions About Leaving Japan

Do I have to file a moving-out notification when I leave Japan? +

You should. The official checklists for foreign residents include a notification of moving out of Japan, filed at the ward office or city office where your residence is registered before you depart. If you are moving to another city inside Japan instead, you file a moving-out notification (転出届) there and a moving-in notification (転入届) at your new address, usually within 14 days of moving in. Exact offices and deadlines differ, so confirm them with your own municipality.

Can Japan Post forward my mail overseas? +

No. Japan Post states plainly that it is unable to forward mail overseas. Its forwarding service redirects mail and parcels from your former address to a new address in Japan, free of charge for one year from the date you submit the relocation form — not from the requested start date. After that year, items are returned to the sender, and you renew by submitting the form again.

What tax do I need to sort out before leaving Japan? +

If you still have tax procedures after you leave, the National Tax Agency says you must either appoint a tax agent who lives in Japan and file the Notification of Tax Agent before departure, or file your final return and pay before you leave. Without an agent, a quasi-final return is filed before departure. Leaving without settling either can attract additional tax and delinquent tax.

How long do I have to claim a lump-sum withdrawal payment? +

Two years. The Japan Pension Service states that non-Japanese members who leave Japan after short coverage periods can apply for a lump-sum withdrawal payment, provided they are no longer covered by National Pension or Employees' Pension Insurance and they file the application within two years of leaving Japan.

What happens to my health insurance when I move or leave? +

If you are enrolled in National Health Insurance, the guidance published by Japanese universities tells you to withdraw from the scheme when you leave Japan: take your insurance card, flight ticket or other proof of your departure date to the municipal office, settle any surplus or shortfall in premiums, and have the expiry date of your card corrected to your departure date. If you are moving within Japan instead, ask both the old and the new office how your enrolment should be moved.
